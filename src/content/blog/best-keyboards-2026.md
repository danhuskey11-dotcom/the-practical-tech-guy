## What to Look for When Buying a Keyboard
<a href="#top" class="article-action">Back to Quick Picks</a>

Before choosing any keyboard, pay particular attention to four things.

### Layout

Keyboard layout has a bigger impact on everyday use than many specifications.

A **full-size keyboard** includes the function row, navigation keys, arrow keys, and a dedicated number pad. That's useful for spreadsheets, accounting, data entry, and other work that involves entering numbers regularly.

A **96% keyboard** keeps most of those keys but compresses the layout to save desk space.

A **75% keyboard** removes the dedicated number pad and compresses the navigation area while generally retaining the function row and arrow keys.

Smaller isn't automatically better.

If you regularly work with numbers, Excel spreadsheets, accounting applications, or data entry, the space saved by removing the number pad may not be worth giving it up.

### Mechanical vs. Low-Profile Keys

Mechanical keyboards use individual mechanical switches underneath their keys.

Different switches can change factors such as:

- Actuation feel
- Key travel
- Sound
- Resistance
- Feedback

Hot-swappable mechanical keyboards such as the Keychron Q6 Ultra 8K and NuPhy Node75 also let you replace compatible switches without soldering.

Low-profile productivity keyboards such as the Logitech MX Keys S take a different approach.

The shorter key travel and lower overall height can feel considerably more familiar to people accustomed to laptops.

Neither design is automatically better.

If you've spent years typing on laptops, moving to a tall mechanical keyboard can actually feel less comfortable at first.

### Wired, Bluetooth, and 2.4 GHz Wireless

Many premium keyboards now support multiple connection types.

**Bluetooth** is convenient because it doesn't require a dedicated USB receiver and can work across computers, tablets, and other supported devices.

**2.4 GHz wireless** uses a dedicated receiver and is particularly useful when responsiveness matters, especially for gaming.

**USB** provides a straightforward wired connection and eliminates wireless battery concerns.

For a permanent desktop setup, having both wired and wireless options gives you useful flexibility.

### Battery Life

Wireless keyboard battery specifications need some context.

Features such as:

- RGB lighting
- Backlighting
- Wireless connection mode
- Sleep settings
- Polling rate

can substantially affect battery life.

Manufacturer battery estimates are useful for comparison, but don't assume you'll get the maximum advertised runtime under every configuration.

A keyboard rated for hundreds of hours with its lighting disabled may last considerably less time with RGB running continuously.

### Hot-Swappable Switches

If you're buying a mechanical keyboard, hot-swapping is worth considering even if you don't intend to replace the switches immediately.

A hot-swappable board gives you the option to change the typing feel later without replacing the entire keyboard.

It can also make replacing an individual failed switch easier.

That doesn't mean everyone needs hot-swapping.

Someone buying a Logitech MX Keys S because they specifically prefer its low-profile typing experience probably isn't looking for that type of customization in the first place.

### Software

Some keyboards rely more heavily on manufacturer software than others.

Software can provide useful features such as:

- Key remapping
- Macros
- RGB configuration
- Application-specific shortcuts
- Firmware updates
- Multi-device features

But software can also add another background application to your computer.

Before choosing a keyboard based on its programmable features, consider whether you're comfortable installing and maintaining the associated software.

### Price

Don't assume spending more automatically improves everyday typing.

A premium mechanical keyboard can provide:

- Better case materials
- Replaceable switches
- Higher polling rates
- Better acoustic treatment
- More customization
- More complex wireless features

Those improvements can absolutely matter to the right person.

But none of them necessarily make email, web browsing, or basic office work better for someone who just needs a functional keyboard.

Buy the capabilities you'll actually use.

- Is the typing feel uncomfortable?

- Do you need a number pad?
- Do you switch between several computers?
- Do you want better gaming responsiveness?
- Do you want mechanical switches?
- Or do you simply need an inexpensive keyboard that reliably works?

Once you answer that question, the right keyboard usually becomes much easier to identify.
